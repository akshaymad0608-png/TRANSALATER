Common Hindi to English Translation Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

By Akshay Mahajan · Updated November 2026

Machine translation of Hindi into English is usually good enough to get the gist. The mistakes that matter are rarely random: they come from a handful of places where Hindi and English work differently. If you know those places, you can rewrite your input in ten seconds and get a much better result.

The examples below are illustrative patterns of what to watch for, not benchmark results. Translation engines change over time, so always read the output yourself rather than assuming any single example behaves identically today.

1. Words that mean two opposite things

The classic case is कल (kal), which means both “yesterday” and “tomorrow”. A human reads the verb tense to decide. A short sentence with no tense clue forces the engine to guess.

  • Risky: कल मीटिंग है। (kal meeting hai) — here the present-tense hai points to “tomorrow”, but a bare word like “कल” on its own gives no signal.
  • Safer: write the date: “27 नवंबर को मीटिंग है” (the meeting is on 27 November).

The same applies to परसों (parson), which means both “the day before yesterday” and “the day after tomorrow”. When a date or time matters, put the actual date in.

2. आप, तुम and तू — three levels of “you”

English has one “you”. Hindi chooses between formal aap, familiar tum and intimate or rude tu. When you translate English into Hindi, the engine has to pick one, and a wrong pick can sound cold, over-familiar or disrespectful.

  • For a customer, a boss, an elder or a stranger, check that the Hindi output uses aap (आप) with verbs like kijiye, boliye.
  • If you see tu-style verbs (kar, bol) in a message to a client, change them before sending.

3. Gender you cannot see

Hindi verbs and adjectives change with the speaker’s and the subject’s gender. “I am going” is main ja raha hoon for a man and main ja rahi hoon for a woman. English gives the engine no clue, so it often defaults to the masculine form.

If you are translating into Hindi on behalf of a woman, read the verb endings (-i / -ee instead of -a) and correct them. Going the other way, Hindi to English, the gender is simply lost, so “वह आ रही है” becomes “she is coming” only because the verb carries it. A sentence without a gendered verb may come out as “he” when the person was a “she”.

4. Idioms and proverbs

Idioms are translated word for word far more often than they should be. “आँखों का तारा” is “the apple of one’s eye”, not “star of the eyes”. “नौ दो ग्यारह होना” means to run away, not “to become nine, two, eleven”. If the output sounds oddly literal, look for an idiom in the source and replace it with plain language: “वह भाग गया” (he ran away).

5. Names, places and brands

Proper names are transliterated, and transliteration is not standardised. “प्रिया” may become Priya, Pria or Preeya. “गुजरात” is Gujarat. When a name appears in a form, an address or a ticket, copy the spelling from an official document rather than from the translator.

6. Mixed Hindi and English (Hinglish)

People write “Kal ka meeting reschedule kar do please”. Mixed input is the hardest case because the engine has to decide which language each word belongs to. Two fixes help:

  • Type Hindi in Devanagari when you can. “कल की मीटिंग फिर से तय कर दीजिए” is far more reliable than the Roman-script version.
  • If you must use Roman script, keep each sentence entirely in one language.

7. Long sentences with dropped subjects

Hindi regularly drops the subject (“गया था” — “had gone” — who?). English cannot. A long chain of clauses with no stated subject is where engines most often attach the wrong “he”, “she” or “they”. Break the text into sentences of one idea each and name the subject at least once per sentence.

A quick rewrite checklist

  1. Replace “कल” and “परसों” with real dates.
  2. Keep one idea per sentence and name the subject.
  3. Swap idioms for plain words.
  4. Use Devanagari rather than Roman script where possible.
  5. Translate the result back into Hindi. If the meaning shifts, the original translation probably did too.
  6. For anything that will be signed, paid for or published, have a fluent speaker read it.

You can try any of these rewrites in the Hindi to English translator or the English to Hindi translator.

Worked examples: before and after

These show the rewrite habit, not guaranteed engine output. Run both versions in the translator and compare.

  • Ambiguous time. Before: “कल आऊँगा”. After: “मैं 27 नवंबर को आऊँगा”. The date removes the yesterday/tomorrow guess and the explicit “मैं” fixes the subject.
  • Idiom. Before: “उसने मुझे उल्लू बनाया”. After: “उसने मुझे धोखा दिया” (he deceived me). The first literally mentions an owl.
  • Dropped subject. Before: “घर पहुँचकर फोन किया और खाना खाया”. After: “वह घर पहुँचा। उसने फोन किया। फिर उसने खाना खाया।” Three short sentences, subject stated each time.
  • Politeness. Before (to a customer): “अपना नंबर बता”. After: “कृपया अपना नंबर बताइए”. The second is polite in Hindi and survives into polite English.

Numbers, units and currency

Indian number formats are a quiet source of errors. Hindi speakers say lakh (1,00,000) and crore (1,00,00,000), while English uses thousand and million. “2.5 लाख” is 250,000, and an engine may render it as “2.5 lakh” or as “250,000”. Both are correct, but if the reader is outside India, convert the figure yourself and write it in full. The same care applies to dates written as day/month/year, to rupee amounts (₹ versus “Rs.”), and to units such as tola or bigha that have no neat English equivalent.

Respect words and family terms

Hindi has separate words for relatives that English folds together: chacha, mama, mausa and phupha can all become “uncle”, and bhabhi is “sister-in-law” only loosely. If the exact relationship matters, in a form or a visa application for example, write it out: “मेरे पिता के छोटे भाई” (my father’s younger brother). Honorific suffixes such as -ji and titles such as shri are usually dropped or left untranslated; decide whether that is acceptable for your reader.

Frequently asked questions

Is the translation from Hindi to English accurate?

For everyday sentences it is usually understandable. Accuracy drops with idioms, ambiguous words, mixed Hindi-English text and long sentences. It is a machine translation, so read it before you rely on it.

Should I type in Devanagari or Roman letters?

Devanagari is more reliable. Roman-script Hindi has no standard spelling, so the engine has to guess which word you mean.

Why does the same sentence give different results at different times?

Translation engines are updated, and results can vary with surrounding words. Treat any single output as a draft.

Can I use it for official documents?

Use it to understand them. For submission to an office, court or embassy, use a certified translator.
