Gujarati, Marathi and Tamil Machine Translation: Limits and Workarounds

By Akshay Mahajan · Updated November 2026

Rosetta supports the major Indian languages, and for everyday messages the results are useful. But machine translation is not equally strong across languages, and Indian regional languages generally get less training data than English, Spanish or French. This guide explains the typical weak points and how to work around them.

These are general characteristics of machine translation, written from our experience building and using translation tools. They are not measured accuracy scores, and quality changes as engines are updated.

Why regional languages are harder

  • Less written material online to learn from, especially for formal, technical and conversational text.
  • Rich grammar: gendered verbs, honorific levels, cases and agglutinative word-building (words made by joining many parts) that English does not have.
  • Dialects and registers. Spoken Gujarati in Surat, Ahmedabad and Kutch differs; Marathi in Pune and Nagpur differs; Tamil in Chennai and Jaffna differs. Engines favour the standard written form.
  • Script differences. Typing in Roman letters (“kem cho”, “kasa kay”, “vanakkam”) is far less reliable than using the native script.

Gujarati

  • Formal and informal “you” (તમે / તું) are easy to confuse. Check which one the output uses for the person you are addressing.
  • Postpositions attach to nouns and the order differs from English; short, simple English input gives the cleanest Gujarati.
  • Business terms, place names and English loanwords are often left in English or spelled inconsistently. Compare with a native speaker’s usage if it is customer-facing.

Marathi

  • Marathi and Hindi share the Devanagari script and many words, which can make the engine slip Hindi words or grammar into Marathi output. Read the result for Hindi forms.
  • Gendered verbs include neuter forms that Hindi lacks. Mistakes here are easy for native readers to spot.
  • Honorific plural usage for respect is common; check it when addressing elders or customers.

Tamil

  • Tamil has a large gap between written (formal) and spoken forms. Engines usually produce the formal written style, which can sound stiff or bookish if used for casual chat.
  • Words are built by adding suffixes, so one Tamil word can correspond to a whole English phrase. Very long input sentences can lose parts.
  • Technical vocabulary is often borrowed or transliterated from English; two engines may spell the same word differently.

Workarounds that work in any of them

  1. Write short, plain source sentences. One idea, subject stated, no idioms.
  2. Use the native script for input where you can.
  3. Translate back. Put the output back through the translator in the reverse direction. If the meaning has changed, rewrite the original.
  4. Keep a glossary of the names, product terms and amounts that must not change, and check them by eye every time.
  5. Have a native reader review anything customer-facing, published or legally relevant.

Try it in the translators for Gujarati, Marathi and Tamil, or go the other way with Gujarati to English, Marathi to English and Tamil to English.

A short checklist for reviewing output you cannot read

If you do not read the target language, you can still catch many problems without a native speaker:

  1. Numbers. Compare every digit with the source. Numbers should be identical or an obvious equivalent.
  2. Names. Names of people, places and products should look the same as in the source (or be an accepted transliteration).
  3. Length. If the output is much shorter than you expect, part of the input may have been dropped. Split the text and translate in pieces.
  4. Back-translation. Translate back to English and compare the meaning.
  5. Second engine. Run the same text through another tool. Where the two disagree, a human should look.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Translating a whole page at once. Long text gives more room for dropped clauses; translate paragraph by paragraph.
  • Translating text that already contains mixed languages and abbreviations. Expand abbreviations first.
  • Trusting a confident-looking result. Fluent output can still be wrong; machine translation tends to read smoothly even when it has changed the meaning.
  • Using literal slogans or jokes. Marketing lines rarely survive machine translation; rewrite them natively.

Writing source text that translates well

Whatever the target language, input written for translation gets better results: use short sentences, avoid phrasal verbs (“sort out”, “follow up”), avoid slang and humour, spell out acronyms, keep one meaning per word (do not use “bank” for both river and money without context), and use consistent terms for product features. This takes a minute and often improves the output more than switching engines.

Frequently asked questions

Is Gujarati or Marathi translation as good as Spanish or French?

Generally less strong, because there is less training data. Check output for anything important.

Can I translate between two Indian languages directly?

Many engines route through English internally, which can add errors. For important text, check the result or translate via English yourself and review each step.

Does it handle dialects?

Mostly standard forms. Dialect words may be left untranslated or misread.
