Before You Translate a Contract, Medical or Legal Document

By Akshay Mahajan · Updated November 2026

Machine translation is one of the most useful tools for understanding a document in a language you do not read. It is also one of the easiest ways to make an expensive mistake when you treat the result as final. This guide separates the two uses.

Use 1: understanding the gist — usually fine

Reading a rental notice, a hospital leaflet, a supplier’s quotation or a visa letter in your own language to understand roughly what it says is exactly what machine translation is good for. Use it freely, but keep the original beside it.

Use 2: acting on the exact words — be careful

Do not rely on a machine translation alone when you will:

  • sign or accept a contract, lease, loan or insurance policy;
  • follow medication, dosage or treatment instructions;
  • submit documents to a court, tax office, embassy or university;
  • publish text under your name or your company’s.

In those cases a single wrong word can change who owes what, which dose to take, or whether an application is accepted.

Where mistakes hide

  • Numbers, dates and currency. Check every figure against the original. Date formats differ (03/04 can be 3 April or 4 March).
  • Modal verbs. “shall”, “may”, “must” and “should” carry different legal weight, and many languages do not distinguish them the same way.
  • Negatives and conditions. “unless”, “except”, “provided that” and double negatives are easily reversed or lost.
  • Defined terms. Contracts use capitalised terms with specific meanings. An engine may translate one defined term two different ways, breaking the document’s internal logic.
  • Legal and medical terminology. Many terms have no exact equivalent across legal systems or in everyday language, and engines choose a plausible word, not the correct term of art.
  • Names and addresses must match your official documents exactly.

A safer workflow

  1. Translate to understand the document. Do not sign yet.
  2. Mark every number, date, deadline, penalty and obligation. Verify each against the original text yourself.
  3. Translate the key clauses back into the original language. If the meaning drifts, flag that clause.
  4. Ask a fluent speaker or a professional to review the flagged parts.
  5. For official submissions, use a certified translator. Many authorities require a certified or sworn translation, and a machine output will be rejected whatever its quality.

Privacy matters too

Pasting a full contract or medical report into any free online translator sends it to a third-party service. If the document is confidential, remove names and identifiers first, or use a professional service under a confidentiality agreement. See what happens to your text when you use an online translator.

When it is worth paying a professional

  • Anything legally binding or submitted to an authority.
  • Medical records, prescriptions and consent forms.
  • Marketing text, packaging and product labelling, where tone and local regulations matter.
  • Anything where the cost of a mistake is higher than the cost of a translator.

Worked examples of how meaning shifts

These are illustrative patterns, not output from a particular engine:

  • “Shall” versus “may”. “The tenant shall pay” (obligation) and “the tenant may pay” (option) can both come out as an ordinary present-tense verb in another language, hiding the difference between a duty and a permission.
  • Notice periods. “Thirty days’ notice” can become “a month’s notice”. These are not the same in February.
  • “Twice daily” versus “every 12 hours”. Medication instructions that sound alike can imply different schedules.
  • “Not less than”. “Not less than 5%” and “not more than 5%” differ by one word and mean opposite things. Check negatives every time.
  • Units. “mg” and “mcg” differ by a factor of 1,000. Never accept a changed unit.

Certified and sworn translation

Many institutions ask for a certified translation: a translation accompanied by the translator’s signed statement that it is complete and accurate. Some countries use sworn translators registered with a court. Requirements differ by country and by office, so ask the receiving authority what it accepts before you order a translation. A machine translation is not accepted as certified, however good it is.

Preparing a document so it translates well

  • Start from a clean digital copy rather than a photo or scan, which introduces recognition errors.
  • Translate tables and lists cell by cell or line by line so numbers stay attached to labels.
  • Keep a glossary of defined terms and use it consistently.
  • Include a note of the document’s purpose for the translator or reviewer.

What a reviewer needs from you

To keep a professional’s time (and cost) down, give them the original, the machine draft, and a list of the parts that worry you. Ask them to review rather than retranslate; for a short document this can be cheaper, but a complex legal text may need a full retranslation.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use machine translation for a rental agreement?

To understand it, yes. Before signing, have the key clauses checked by a person who reads both languages.

Is Rosetta suitable for medical information?

It can help you understand general information. It is not a substitute for a clinician or a qualified medical interpreter.

What if two translators disagree?

That is a signal that the source text is ambiguous. Resolve the ambiguity with the author of the original.
